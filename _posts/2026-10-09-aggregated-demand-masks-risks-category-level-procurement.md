Procurement decisions increasingly hinge on data-driven insights. But the way market intelligence is structured often determines its practical value. For category leaders, combining different demand signals into a single, aggregated view is common practice—yet this approach may be weakening sourcing strategies instead of strengthening them.

Blended demand data—whether integrating multiple regions, sales channels, or end-users—tends to smooth out meaningful volatility. The result: missed opportunities, misjudged risks, and lost negotiation leverage, just as sourcing teams seek to control costs and improve supply resilience.

## Aggregation Blurs the Edges of Opportunity

At the center of many category level procurement strategies is the demand forecast. Market research vendors typically deliver these as consolidated figures. The rationale is simplicity: tidy, single-number estimates to model demand, allocate contracts, or benchmark supplier proposals.

Yet buyer power and supplier risk rarely move in a uniform direction across every market segment. Consider a category like industrial adhesives. Demand from consumer electronics, automotive, and building sectors each behaves differently. Macro trends—such as electric vehicle adoption or construction slowdowns—pull on specific subsegments, not always in lockstep.

When procurement teams act on aggregate figures, they effectively plan to the average. This can disguise upswings in some regions or tail-offs elsewhere. A region experiencing rapid growth could offer short-term leverage to secure volume-based discounts or favorable contract terms, but that edge is lost if procurement assumes uniform demand.

## Channel Divergence Is Actionable, Not Noise

Regional or channel-level divergences are often dismissed as noise in aggregated reporting. That assumption makes sense only if the procurement strategy covers one, homogeneous market. In practice, category managers often spread spend across multiple geographies or end-user types, each with distinct cost and supply dynamics.

For instance, retail food packaging demand can surge in Asian cities during festival seasons while remaining flat in North America. Wholesale hardware suppliers may see robust sales through ecommerce even as in-store volumes shrink. Sourcing teams treating these movements as statistical outliers overlook actionable information.

The true procurement opportunity is to separate the signal from the noise—recognizing that volatility across segments is often the best predictor of cost pressure or supplier constraints. An aggregated view will always underplay these variances, leading to decisions that are misaligned with on-the-ground realities.

## Mispricing Risk: The Invisible Cost of Blending

One of the strongest arguments for disaggregation comes from risk management. Procurement teams responsible for widespread operations must hedge not against average risk, but against outliers. Aggregated demand smooths demand variability, making periods of strain or oversupply appear less severe than they are in any one segment.

Look at medical diagnostics: point-of-care test demand exploded in some regional health systems during specific phases of the pandemic, but other geographies lagged, or even declined, at the same time. Bulk demand analysis pointed to moderate, consistent growth. Teams buying to the average repeatedly faced stockouts or excess inventory, paying a premium to recover.

Price index tracking for construction materials often reveals wild swings locally—driven by infrastructure schedules or weather disruptions—while national aggregates appear stable. Basing contract terms, inventory buffers, or hedging strategies on the blended number invites exposure to hidden volatility. When a procurement group misses these divergences, it effectively funds risk premiums rather than seizing the chance to negotiate from strength.

## Negotiation Leverage Lives in the Details

Supplier markets are rarely monolithic. Large vendors, aware of their exposure to specific regions or customer types, set terms and capacity allocations accordingly. Procurement teams with an internal view of those micro-markets can anticipate—and sometimes shape—the conversation.

For example, if an electronics assembler knows channel demand for lithium batteries is set to spike in Southeast Asia, it can pre-commit volumes or set escalation clauses when negotiating with suppliers predominantly exposed there. If, instead, the procurement plan is anchored by global average demand, the buyer loses this window.

Specificity is also critical in indirect spend. Facilities maintenance or corporate services may represent a consolidated category on the balance sheet, but the underlying demand drivers—operational uptime, local regulation, industry cycle—differ across sites. Sourcing to the blended average means repeatedly overpaying in some locations, under-supplying in others.

## Category Level Procurement: Why Disaggregation Is Strategic

Disaggregating demand signals forces procurement to acknowledge differences that matter. This is less about creating additional complexity for its own sake, and more about exposing actionable divergences that aggregate data conceals.

Analytically, this approach is only practical if paired with decision rules: what actions to take when subsegment variance exceeds a threshold, how to design contracts with flexibility, and when to pool versus separate sourcing events. The output is not always split contracts or micro-negotiations—it might be a revised risk premium, new hedging approach, or differentiated supplier qualification process.

Furthermore, disaggregation supports internal alignment, giving finance, operations, and supply chain stakeholders a shared view of where risk and opportunity truly reside. This, in turn, makes the business case for flexibility—pre-buying inventory where demand risk is high, or standardizing in markets where volatility really is low.

## Takeaway: Restructure Data to Reshape Results

The imperative for sharper category decisions is not going away. As supply chains globalize further and end-market volatility becomes the norm, acting on the average is increasingly a losing proposition.

Category managers should challenge data providers to deliver not only headline figures, but also the segmentation that lets teams detect—and act on—divergence. Where market research only offers an aggregated view, treat outputs as a prompt for deeper inquiry, not a final answer.

The difference between a smooth average and a jagged, actionable demand curve is the difference between buying at market and buying with foresight.
