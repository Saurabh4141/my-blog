Executives making sourcing, investment, or go-to-market decisions often turn to market share data for guidance. Yet, when suppliers use overlapping channel strategies—spanning direct sales, traditional distribution, and hybrids—the headline market share figures often misrepresent real commercial power. This challenge has become more acute as many sectors accelerate multi-channel strategies to reach fragmented customers, meet margin targets, or respond to procurement shifts.

Readers building procurement or product strategies require a deeper understanding of how channel overlap alters market share reports—often exaggerating or hiding true supplier position. Without interrogating underlying channel constructs, it is easy to misread data and misjudge where leverage really sits.

## Overlapping Distribution Blurs Market Boundaries

Channel overlap occurs when a supplier uses multiple parallel paths to the end user—such as selling both through resellers/distributors and directly to key accounts. Hybrid approaches, where a supplier or its channel partners cover the same customers, are now standard in many sectors: industrial equipment, medtech, packaged goods, even IT infrastructure. This overlap makes it difficult to allocate end-customer sales unambiguously into a single segment or supplier.

In such environments, headline market share is more a function of how customer spend is attributed than an absolute measure of commercial foothold. The problem is not simply one of accounting preference—misallocation can distort supplier perceived significance, affect procurement negotiations, and reset competitor benchmarks.

## Channel Segmentation Choices: Overstatement or Understatement

Whether a firm appears to dominate or lag in market share can hinge on how market research segments and aggregates sales:

- Double-counting risk: Sales facilitated by resellers, especially when supported by supplier-led key account teams or joint bids, may be attributed both as direct and indirect revenue. This inflates total market size and a supplier’s share.
- Exclusion risk: Research that defines the 'direct' market narrowly may omit hybrid deals where both direct sales and partner fulfillment are involved, understating a supplier's presence.
- Channel bias: Some reports aggregate all sales to the final customer regardless of route, masking the power (and margin position) of distributors versus direct sales organizations. Others show only original supplier sales, ignoring distributor stock-in, return flows, or non-exclusive third-party channels.

The net effect is that 'share' can swing by several percentage points or more, solely based on channel coding. For procurement, these swings change the perceived risk and negotiating stance. For product teams, they obscure which go-to-market models are winning at the segment level.

## Multiple Channel Strategies Increase Attribution Complexity

Suppliers have rational reasons for mixed channel strategies:

- Reach: Some customer segments are best served by high-touch direct sales, while others are better matched with local distribution.
- Margin optimization: Differences in channel cost structures mean certain products or deal types favor direct or indirect sales.
- Channel conflict management: Hybrids enable suppliers to maintain price controls or launch new products without fully bypassing incumbent partners.
- Geographical coverage: Partners can extend reach into regions where building a direct salesforce is uneconomical.

The result is that, at the transaction level, a single end customer may interact with both the supplier’s direct team and its partners. This interleaving—joint sales visits, hybrid fulfillment, channel-led contracts with direct support—makes it extremely challenging for third-party market research to assign the resulting revenue cleanly.

## Channel Overlap Market Share Challenges: A Hypothetical Worked Example

Consider the following simplified case. (Illustrative figures only, for method demonstration—not market data.)

A manufacturer sells industrial controllers via three routes:

- Direct to large OEMs
- Through national distributors
- A hybrid model, where its own field team supports local system integrators executing bids

Suppose total reported sales to the end market come to $600M, distributed as follows:

- Direct: $250M
- Indirect (distributors): $200M
- Hybrid: $150M

However, $60M of the hybrid sales are supported by both the direct team and fulfilled via distributor contracts. If researchers segregate hybrid from direct and indirect, total supplier share may seem lower or higher depending on allocation:

- **If hybrid sales are all counted as supplier-direct**: Supplier appears to hold $400M ($250M direct + $150M hybrid).
- **If hybrid sales are assigned 50/50 to direct and distributor**: Direct share is $325M and indirect is $275M.
- **If only cleanly attributable sales are counted, and hybrid deals are excluded**: Supplier’s market share is understated at just $450M out of $600M, masking its true coverage.

Applied to multiple suppliers, with varying channel mixes, these attribution choices visibly shift reported shares—sometimes more than underlying changes in competitive activity.

## Implications for Procurement and Commercial Strategy

Channel overlap misleads strategy in several predictable ways. First, it can overstate the scale or competitive influence of suppliers who aggressively use joint channel models but whose sales are counted repeatedly. Second, it can underrepresent firms who excel at supporting buyers across blended channels, if the research methodology sets a high bar for 'clean' attribution.

Procurement professionals deciding which suppliers merit greater consideration or pose viable alternatives can be misled about true supplier spread or depth. Product and strategy teams looking to calibrate investment in particular channel programs risk calibrating against mismeasured benchmarks, distorting the focus of resources and initiatives.

## A Framework for Interrogating Channel Overlap in Market Share Figures

To mitigate these distortions, decision-makers should apply this practical, repeatable process:

1. **Assess Channel Definitions:** Examine how the market research segments direct, indirect, and hybrid sales. Look for double-counting or ambiguous hybrid allocations.
2. **Identify Attribution Mechanisms:** Determine how the researcher assigns shared or blended sales—by contract origin, fulfillment route, or both. Ask to see the underlying constructs or flow diagrams.
3. **Test Supplier Comparability:** Check whether all major suppliers are treated symmetrically; differing channel mixes create artificial share swings if treated inconsistently.
4. **Adjust Benchmarks:** Reframe supplier positions by recasting shares with more conservative or consistent channel attribution if possible.
5. **Align Procurement or Product Analysis with True Channel Reach:** Ground decisions in channel reach as actually experienced by end customers, not as modeled in a simplified share table.

This structured approach, applied rigorously, reduces the risk of strategic miscalculation driven by channel overlap artefacts.
