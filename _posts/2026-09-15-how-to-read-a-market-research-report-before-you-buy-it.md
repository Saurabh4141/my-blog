Most buyers judge a market research report by its executive summary. That is the one section written to be persuasive, and it is the last place the weaknesses will show.

## Read the methodology before the findings

The methodology section tells you what the numbers can actually support. Three things matter more than the rest:

- **Sample size and who was sampled.** Four hundred responses from procurement leads is a different claim from four hundred responses collected from an open web panel.
- **The date the data was collected**, not the date the report was published. A twelve-month gap between the two is common and rarely advertised.
- **Whether the market sizing is bottom-up or top-down.** Top-down sizing starts from a large number and divides it. Bottom-up starts from units and prices and adds them up. They routinely disagree by a factor of two, and only one of them can be checked.

If a report will not tell you these three things plainly, the findings cannot be audited, and an unauditable number is not worth paying for.

## Check what the forecast is actually built on

A five-year forecast is an assumption wearing a number's clothes. The useful question is not "what is the CAGR" but "what has to stay true for that CAGR to hold".

Good reports state those assumptions explicitly: adoption rates, pricing pressure, regulatory timelines. Weak ones present a smooth curve and leave you to guess. When the assumptions are listed, you can disagree with them and adjust — which is the whole point of buying the analysis rather than the conclusion.

## Segmentation is where the value usually hides

The headline market size is the least useful number in most reports. It is the segmentation that tells you whether your part of the market is growing while the total shrinks, or the reverse.

Before buying, look at the table of contents and ask whether the segments match how you actually go to market. A report segmented by region when your constraint is channel will give you an impressive document and no decisions.

## What to ask for before you pay

- A sample section from the body of the report, not the summary.
- The respondent profile for any primary research.
- Confirmation of which figures are modelled and which are measured.

Any credible publisher will answer all three. The ones that will not are usually reselling someone else's model, and you can often find the original for less.
