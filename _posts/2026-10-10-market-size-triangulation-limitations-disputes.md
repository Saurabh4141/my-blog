Market size estimates sit at the center of many investment, procurement, and strategy decisions. As businesses reassess categories—from emerging technology to established industrials—teams increasingly turn to triangulation: combining multiple third-party market studies to arrive at a more reliable figure. In theory, this should reduce uncertainty. In practice, it often introduces more ambiguity, particularly when the underlying sources are not methodologically aligned.

## Triangulation’s Appeal Depends on Overlooked Assumptions

Triangulation appears objective. Multiple numbers, independently sourced, supposedly tell a more rigorous story than any single point of view. Procurement leads and corporate strategists expect that blending sources will reveal a consensus or, at worst, bracket the true size of a market. In reality, this method assumes more common ground among data providers than actually exists.

Underlying this approach is the belief that differences in published estimates stem mostly from data recency or minor definitional drift, not from hard-to-reconcile methodological gaps. Teams routinely underestimate how divergent source studies can be—in terms of definitions, time horizons, research methodologies, and even the segments counted.

## Divergent Segment Definitions Render Direct Comparisons Unreliable

Market size triangulation fails most visibly where segment definitions are not standardized. What one provider groups under “industrial IoT,” for example, may exclude edge computing or focus only on hardware, while another folds in software and services. Even seasoned analysts may struggle to untangle these distinctions from the published report summaries available to procurement teams.

This misalignment is especially acute in multi-layered supply chains and cross-cutting technology markets, where traditional category boundaries evolve faster than taxonomy updates. Comparing “like with like” often becomes guesswork.

The impact: two reports may both cover the same market in name, yet point to figures separated by a multiple rather than a margin. Aggregating or averaging such numbers yields a pseudo-figure unmoored from any consistent underlying reality.

## Methodology Mismatch Undermines Perceived Objectivity

A second, less visible challenge lies in methodological differences. Some sources model demand top-down—starting from macroeconomic indicators and working down—to calculate size, while others aggregate disclosed revenue data (a bottom-up approach). Others blend survey results, channel checks, proprietary data, or triangulate themselves, layering on further assumptions.

Lumping together results derived from fundamentally different methods does not average out error; it instead introduces new, less visible layers of uncertainty. A decision-maker who blends a conservative, bottom-up estimate with an aggressive, top-down projection has not found a middle ground. Rather, they have combined the biases of both, while masking the assumptions that shaped each one.

## Recency Gaps Create False Signals of Trend or Stability

Timing gaps further confuse the picture. Third-party reports frequently lag actual market developments by quarters or even years, especially in fast-evolving sectors. A dataset assembled in early 2023 and labeled as representing the “current” market is already outpaced by mid-2024 conditions.

When teams triangulate across sources of varying recency, they inadvertently compare out-of-date market conditions with figures reflecting more current realities. An apparent trend or consensus could simply reflect a lag between research cycles. Updating all estimates to a common basis year, or adjusting for market growth, is possible in theory but rarely straightforward, as growth rates are themselves model-dependent.

## Statistical Aggregation Is Not Conceptual Reconciliation

There is a persistent tendency to treat triangulation as a statistical exercise: plug published market figures into a spreadsheet, discard outliers, and average the rest. This process feels disciplined, yet it sidesteps conceptual work that is harder to automate—namely, aligning definitions, verifying methodological consistency, and accounting for significant structural differences in the market view.

Without this conceptual due diligence, the average of three third-party estimates is no more authoritative than the single figure perceived as most robust. Caution is warranted: statistical aggregation cannot substitute for synthesis.

### Table: How Triangulation Can Mask Rather Than Resolve Differences

| Factor                 | Typical Source of Misalignment    | Impact on Triangulation          |
|------------------------|-----------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Segment Definitions    | Varying inclusions/exclusions     | Aggregation distorts market size |
| Methodologies          | Top-down vs. bottom-up approaches | Invisible bias in results        |
| Data Recency           | Different collection periods      | Illusory consensus or trends     |
| Structural Assumptions | Regional/global boundaries        | Overlap or double-counting       |

## Better Sizing Requires Alignment, Not More Data

The urge to triangulate is understandable: no single vendor or analyst is infallible, and procurement and strategy outcomes often demand defensible numbers. But adding more sources in isolation does not close the gap between disparate estimates. Only explicit reconciliation—through segment definition alignment, comparable methodologies, and shared assumptions—moves the process forward.

When disagreements persist, the best course is often to decompose the estimates—identify what is differently counted, where the timeframes diverge, or why one approach yields systematically higher or lower results. This can mean direct outreach to research publishers, reconstructing approach notes from methodological appendices, and mapping each estimate to an internal model tailored for the buying or investing decision at hand.

## Next Steps: Establish a Reconciliation Framework

Executives and product teams should resist pressure to average third-party market sizes without first interrogating the conceptual compatibility of the inputs. When engaging in sizing efforts, set explicit criteria:

- Require documentation of segment inclusions and exclusions.
- Insist on transparency regarding data collection periods and methodologies.
- Map all sources into a consistent category and time framework before any aggregation.
- Accept that, at times, ‘bracketing’ the plausible range is more honest than pinpointing a pseudo-precise consensus.

Triangulation serves as a useful prompt for discussion, not a shortcut to accuracy. In a market environment defined by overlapping categories and shifting boundaries, only foundational alignment—not more data—builds consensus fit for decision-making.
