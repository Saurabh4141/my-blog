Market share rankings drive budgets, incentive plans, and vendor qualification lists. But the calculation behind those percentages quietly depends on evolving decisions about what belongs in, or out of, the category itself. 

As product definitions, use-cases, and regulatory criteria change, so does the denominator by which winners and laggards are measured. For procurement and product teams, failing to track these framing shifts introduces real decision risk—masking structural market moves as apparent reversals, or concealing an incumbent’s loss of share behind a redefined leaderboard.

## Market Share Calculation Is Not Neutral to Category Definitions

Market share calculation appears to be an objective tracking of each player’s proportion within a market. In fact, the underlying segmentation logic drives the apparent composition of the market and the movement of competitors. When analysts or standards bodies change inclusion or exclusion criteria—such as whether hybrid vehicles, e-bikes, or non-physician-administered diagnostics count toward a market—historical comparisons of market share become discontinuous.

This effect is especially acute in sectors rapidly intersected by technology (e.g., automotive powertrains, consumer electronics, healthcare diagnostics) or regulation (e.g., food labeling, bio-based materials). A new product subtype, or a shift in what counts as a compliant offering, can distort growth rates or cause the sudden rise or fall of major suppliers on published rankings—often due less to intrinsic competition than to what is, or is not, being measured each year.

## Consequences for Teams Relying on Share Trends

For procurement and product teams, using published market share data as a basis for sourcing, contracting, or development roadmaps, these definitional changes can trigger missteps. Apparent growth may be the result of a new subcategory being bundled in; a supplier’s sudden ascendance may be due to widened inclusion criteria rather than their own improved operational execution. 

Failing to recognize this exposes the organization to strategic errors:

- Overstating or understating supplier concentration risk
- Misidentifying which competitors are gaining at the expense of others
- Designing products for features that appear to be gaining share, only because they are newly included
- Negotiating pricing or minimums on skewed perceptions of market scale or share

## What Drives Shifts in Category Boundaries?

Segmentation logic evolves for three principal reasons:

1. **Emergence of New Product Subtypes**: New technologies or business models create ambiguous overlaps (e.g., meal kits between prepared foods and grocery, ride-hailing platforms as transit or tech).
2. **Regulatory or Standards Evolution**: Rule changes redefine, for example, what must be labeled as a medical device or which energy systems qualify as renewable.
3. **Market Research Methodology Updates**: Analysts update inclusion/exclusion criteria for greater comparability, more granular tracking, or to align with client feedback.

Each mechanism alters which revenues, units, or companies are counted. This changes the total market baseline, as well as each participant’s measured share—even with unchanged underlying business performance.

## Detecting Category Redefinition in Market Reporting

To avoid acting on spurious market share trends, teams must actively monitor for three signs:

- **Sudden unexplained shifts in reported market size or leaderboards** (absent corresponding news of company exits/entries or underlying demand shifts)
- **Footnotes or appendices in research noting methodology changes, resegmentation, or category expansions/contractions**
- **Discrepancies between different analyst firms’ published numbers in the same timeframe**

Where these signals appear, trend comparisons across periods should be considered at risk of discontinuity.

## Adjusting Internal Analyses for Shifting Category Definitions

Genuinely comparable trend analysis requires standardizing the inclusion criteria across time. Teams should:

- **Request or reconstruct historical market metrics under the current segmentation logic** when available from research providers
- **Restate supplier or segment figures to exclude or include relevant subcategories for visibility on true like-for-like trends**
- **Qualitatively annotate share shifts that are attributable to redefinition, not commercial activity**

Procurement and commercial evaluation models should embed version control for segmentation rules used in each analysis, tying results to their category basis as clearly as to their data sources.

## Hypothetical Worked Example: Adjusting Market Share After a Category Expansion

To illustrate the effect and adjustment, consider this purely hypothetical example.

Suppose a refrigeration market has two leading companies, A and B. Traditionally, the reported market includes only home refrigerators. Now, a new research revision adds compact refrigerators (dorm-size units) to the category definition starting in 2024. Market size and company revenues are as follows (all figures arbitrary):

|                | 2023 (Old Definition) | 2023 (Restated) | 2024 (New Definition) |
|----------------|----------------------|-----------------|----------------------|
| Total market   | $1,000M              | $1,200M         | $1,250M              |
| Co. A Revenue  | $350M                | $360M           | $370M                |
| Co. B Revenue  | $250M                | $260M           | $265M                |

If a stakeholder calculates Co. A's share:

- 2023 as originally reported: $350M / $1,000M = 35%
- 2024 with new definition: $370M / $1,250M = 29.6%

This looks like a drastic loss of share. But restating 2023 under the new logic (including compact fridges): $360M / $1,200M = 30%. The real change is negligible—most change is definitional, not market-driven.

Teams that spot the change, request or reconstruct historical figures under the new rule, and compare like-with-like can avoid overreacting to the apparent decline.

## Stepwise Approach: Handling Market Share Calculation When Definitions Shift

1. **Screen recent share tables and market sizes for abrupt jumps unrelated to known commercial events.**
2. **Cross-compare definitions, footnotes, and temporal coverage with prior-year reports for signs of resegmentation.**
3. **When a shift is detected, seek restated earlier-period data using updated segmentation, either from the report provider or via logical back-calculation.**
4. **Contextualize and annotate all share movements with notes on whether changes are operational or definitional.**
5. **Revise internal procurement, budgeting, or product plans where necessary to align with corrected (apples-to-apples) share trajectories.**

## Key takeaways

- Annotate all internal market share tracking with explicit category definitions used in each period.
- Prioritize business decisions based on share trends only where segmentation criteria are stable across time.
- Where possible, negotiate with data providers for restated historical data under the current category logic.
- Design incentive structures and risk models to withstand non-market-related share volatility due to segmentation shifts.
